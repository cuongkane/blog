This blog doesn't show you how to become a good tech lead for every team;
it just gives you some of the characteristics and things that a TL needs to have (but not all of them).

**Disclaimer:** I haven't had much experience in a team lead position yet (less than one
 year at the time this blog was written, Oct 4, 2026). This blog is just my collection
of what I've read in well-known books (*The Manager's Path* by Camille Fournier) and my observations of good tech leads (in my opinion).

## Overview

In the software career path, there are two different paths that every experienced developer
needs to choose between when they reach the senior level: create more impact through technical
expertise to solve big, challenging problems, or lead people to meet the company's goals.
Becoming a technical lead is a good move, but it requires expanding your work and interests from
pure technical work to managing people, dealing with higher levels to protect your people, and making everyone around you better.

These are my lessons learned from multiple sources: books and discussions with experienced team leads/managers,
put together as a complete guide to the essential things that a team lead needs to acquire or prepare.

I'll walk you through these sections:

- **Prerequisites**: The gap between the best coder to an ideal team lead
- **Philosophy:** Learning from practice is the deepest way to learn something, but it takes multiple years to build up.
  Therefore, this section gives you the mindset of well-known leaders that you can reference and build into your own thinking and reasoning.
- **Actions:** Specific actions that a team lead should take to build up the team.
- **Tips**: Practical tips to build up the team with respect and trust.

## Prerequisites

- High Accountability: Be responsible for the whole team when issues happen or during firefighting (act as a commander who solves the problem by utilizing the power of the team, instead of as a solo problem solver).
- Grasp essential things: Understand the architecture and functionality of the team.
- Be a team player: work on boring issues and tasks that can improve or fix the process.
- Lead technical decisions: determine which decisions can be made by the TL and which ones cannot.

## Philosophy

### Goal

Your goal changes from "how do I deliver faster" to "how do I make the team deliver faster,"
in order to solve bigger problems for the company/customer, maximize the company's revenue, and help your team grow resiliently.

### Characteristics

Be open-minded: understand your team members, respect their thoughts, and build connections with them.

Balance upper management and your team members. The split should be about 70-30, and which side gets the 70 depends on the workload and the company's situation:

- **When things are running well** and nothing is critical for the company, lean toward the team (70% team, 30% company).
  If the team has been pushing for a long time and is already tired, give team members spare time to relax.
- **When the situation gets worse**, for example when the project needs to scale quickly to resolve financial issues or
  something else critical for the company, lean toward the company (70% company, 30% team). Follow upper management's push
  and go through it with the team, but reserve some time or activities to keep the team happy.

## Actions

### Feedback

- Negative feedback should be raised early so that the member can change as soon as possible. Don't wait for a convenient time.
- Praise should be public and criticism should be private.
- Asking members for feedback and receiving it gently is a perfect way to improve your weaknesses and show respect to them.
- Help members understand the value of their work, even when it isn't glamorous. When the team knows the value their work
  contributes to the end goals, they ask more precise questions and drive things in the right direction together with the team lead.

### 1-1 Meetings

- They allow the manager to notice the member's energy level.
  - Ask for a happiness score for the period being discussed so that the member can raise issues more easily.
  - Ask questions like: "On a scale of 1 to 10, what happiness score would you give the last period?"
- Respect the member's life: spend a few minutes talking about life when you meet to warm up the meeting and create a relaxed time for both of you.
- Speak privately, and have an agenda for the things that need to be discussed.
- Schedule them periodically and predictably so that everyone can plan for them.
- This meeting is not a status meeting; status can be discussed via chat or email. This meeting helps the TL and members understand each other better and build trust.

### Training

- Provide members with training and other resources for career growth.
- Advocate for members' promotions and help get them approved. Managers cannot guarantee promotions, but
  they should know how to help members grow and outperform (with tangible impact) so that they can advance.

### Communication

- Represent the team in front of others and take on the communication overhead, but also give the team chances to speak up and present. Be the note-taker and feedback giver during those sessions to help your team grow faster.

### Mentoring

- Listen carefully and be patient. Repeat the hard problem reported by the member in your own words to make sure you understand it correctly, and respect the member's thoughts.
- Communicate clearly.
- Build up a network for new hires to help them get used to the new environment quickly.

## Tips

### Stop being the best coder; delegate tasks

I used to be the best coder in many teams that I joined. Watching someone work too slowly made me nervous about whether they could finish and release on time.
But we must step back and act as the backup person, supporting our teammates to accomplish the task and trusting that they can do it, instead of doing it with our own hands.
The team won't be able to grow if you do everything yourself, and you will burn out as well, because you'll need to do a lot of coding (usually with dependencies on other things) while also handling team lead responsibilities.
Therefore, delegate first. Only take a task yourself when it's actually needed and there is no one available to delegate it to.

### Room for Mistakes and Experimentation

- Allow members to make mistakes on acceptable things so they can understand those things better. You won't be able to convince all of them to follow what you want.
- For example, when you propose a new tool, approach, or design, the team may not be able to adopt or accept it at that time.
  You can let them do it the way they would like, so that they learn their lessons better and respect your ideas later.
- Don't force everyone to always follow your direction. That creates a passive team that always follows your directions, without innovation or proactivity in solving problems.
  This is the hardest one, because we won't have many chances or much capacity to let team members fail.

### Delegate outcomes, not tasks

To avoid micromanagement, you should clarify the outcome with the team instead of the specific tasks, because team members may have different ways
to accomplish the outcome. If you define the tasks too specifically with technical guidelines (let's say for experienced members), it doesn't give your teammates a chance to
propose a solution (so the solution can't be improved), and it creates redundant back-and-forth because the solution you give may not work (you don't have the whole day to write overly detailed tasks with 100% precise solutions).

### Make team decisions instead of individual decisions

Prefer consensus over compromise. For solutions that will persist in the long term and affect the whole team, schedule a meeting to reach consensus on the solution.
Your output should be the team's output instead of your individual ideas (you should be the person who gives the final decision of the team instead).

### Shielding the team

You should shield the team when they make mistakes or when they receive criticism or hard questions from other stakeholders or managers.
It helps you build credibility with the team, and working with them will be easier.
The problem can also come from you, when you don't manage your teammates efficiently.
So you should shield them.

### Setting clear expectations

I've had many discussions with friends who complain that their team lead doesn't know a good way to, or cannot, create opportunities for them to grow.
Team members and the team lead should have clear expectations of each other so that a win-win is established and the team can go a long way together.
For ambitious devs who would like to be promoted, set aside time to create a specific plan for them (with milestones) to help them achieve it.
For chill devs who don't want to be promoted and just want to explore more things, create opportunities for them to explore.
For devs who are not in a good situation, plan a tolerable workload for them (for a certain time) to help them get through the hard time.

## Conclusion

To sum up, being a team lead isn't easy.
It requires a deep (or deep enough) technical understanding, knowledge of the company's vision and goals, and the
ability to be kind to the team or to raise complaints with them when needed to ensure they contribute in the right way.
